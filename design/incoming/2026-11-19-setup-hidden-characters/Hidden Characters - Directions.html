The server-setup screen’s hidden-character warning and fix button

Ravilo — phone, Android TV and the desktop window. Drawn in this repository, 2026-11-19. One recommended direction, one drawn and not recommended, and three decisions that are not mine to make.

What is fixed before the round starts

Everything the spec already settles. The round draws it and does not reopen it:

  • The field stays one line. The owner said so and FR-4 repeats it.
  • The warning sits directly under the field and holds two things: one sentence naming what is wrong, and the address as typed with each hidden character drawn as a visible mark in the warning colour.
  • The sentence is one of two — This address has a hidden line break or This address has a hidden space — and the round does not write a third.
  • One button fixes it and names the result: Use jelly.example.net.
  • While the warning shows, Connect is disabled (FR-6), in the disabled state the screen already has.
  • On a TV the D-pad order is field → fix button → Connect, and Up reverses it.
  • The screen around it is unchanged: the wordmark, the prompt, the field, Connect, and the Apps row where DEB-4912’s home-screen hook puts one.

What was left to the round: where the button sits relative to the warning line, which mark glyphs to use, what colour the warning is, and what the mark line does when it runs out of width.

The label Gets the whole column and wraps. Gets a fixed share of one row. At 412 dp the Faroese Brúka jelly.example.net breaks mid-word, and so does the English one — see B-phone-linebreak-kbd-up.

A is recommended, and the first reason is the only one that is not taste: FR-5 fixes a vertical D-pad order, and A is that order drawn as a column. The rest follows from the label being unbounded — it carries the cleaned address, so it is as wide as the address is.

The decisions the drawing made

DecisionWhat the round drewWhy
The mark for a line break U+00B6 ¶, the pilcrow — not FR-4’s U+23CE ⏎ Measured, not assumed. glyph-probe renders each candidate in all three self-hosted faces: a codepoint a subset really carries gets three different advance widths, one none of them carries gets three identical ones. U+0041 measures 50 / 76 / 63. U+23CE measures 100 / 100 / 100 — it is in none of our faces and draws from whatever fallback the device has, in a weight and a shape we do not control. U+00B6 measures 53.9 / 63 / 58 and is ours.
The mark for any other hidden character U+00B7 ·, as FR-4 says Measured 23.9 / 26 / 19. It is in all three faces and needs no decision.
The mark is washed The glyph in the warning colour, on an 18 % wash of the same colour, 3 dp radius A bare mark reads as a character of the address, which is the one thing it must not be. The wash says “this is not in your text, this is where your text went wrong” without a second colour or a second weight.
The address around the mark is not warn The address stays --ink-soft; only the mark takes the colour FR-4 asks for the mark “in the warning colour”. Drawing the whole line that way satisfies the sentence and defeats its purpose: the viewer is looking for one character, and a string that is entirely warn has nothing in it to find. See mark-colour.
The mark line is monospaced JetBrains Mono 500, 14 sp Our code-and-ID face by mandate, and one cell per character is what makes a · between two words land exactly where the space is.
The rail, not a card 3 dp rail in the column’s left margin The shipped idiom for “something about this needs saying” is exactly this: .dplaynote .pn-bar and .ep-playnote both draw a 3 px rail in --warn. The setup column holds no cards at all, so a card here would be a new component for one sentence. The rail sits in the margin so the sentence, the mark line and the button share a left edge with the field and Connect.
The fix button is an outline 1 dp border in the warning colour, no fill, 42 dp against Connect’s 52 It is the recovery, not the goal. Filling it would give the screen two primary buttons, one of which is disabled.
Its focus is the ring, not the inverted fill The 2 dp --ring Connect already uses; the border takes the ring colour too The house has two focus treatments. The inverted fill belongs to chips, nav items and pills; this is a button in a form, and the two buttons above and below it use the ring.

Three things that are open

1. What the mark line does when it runs out of width needs a brief

FR-4 says the address is shown “on one line”. mark-line-width measures how much line there is: 412 dp on the phone, 480 on the TV, 440 on the desktop — 57 monospaced characters at the narrowest, against the brief’s 25. The brief’s address does not overflow anywhere, so no frame in this round can show the overflow honestly: the brief names one address and one trailer, and inventing a longer one is the thing the stand-in rule exists to stop.

The rule still has to be decided, because a pasted address is not bounded — a chat app’s trailer can be a sentence. The round draws the mark line as wrapping, break-anywhere, and recommends that over the two alternatives: clipping can hide the very mark the line exists to show, and a horizontally scrolling line is unreachable with a D-pad. If the overflow is to be drawn, the next brief needs to name the long address.

2. The glyph is a change to FR-4 spec amendment

FR-4 names U+23CE by codepoint. The measurement says that codepoint is not in any face we ship. Changing it to U+00B6 changes what a shipped spec promises, so it is a dated amendment inside the spec’s own file, hand-written — not something a drawing can decide on its own.

3. Arrival focus can land on a disabled Connect not this round’s

Read in ServerSetupScreen.kt on 2026-11-19: LaunchedEffect(Unit) { if (host.isNotBlank()) connectFR.requestFocus() }. With FR-3 reading back a saved address and FR-6 disabling Connect while a warning shows, a TV arriving at this screen from Wrong server? with a dirty saved address focuses a control that does nothing, with the fix button one press up. Nothing in the drawing fixes that; it is a line in the Kotlin, and it belongs on the task rather than in a picture.
