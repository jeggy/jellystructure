What a highlighted title tells you

Right now, moving the D-pad across a Ravilo row tells you almost nothing new. A focused tile is a 210×315 poster, a title, and one line: year · first genre. Everything else the library holds — the synopsis, the other three genres, the runtime, whether there's Danish audio, how far into season 2 you are — costs a Select press and a full page load.

On a 10-foot screen the main activity isn't watching, it's deciding. So the question this document answers is: how much can a title say while you are merely looking at it, and what does that cost in bytes, in frames, and in D-pad presses.

Three framings, and they pull apart

Browsing is deciding

The row should answer "what is this?" on its own. Information near the tile, no navigation, no commitment. → A

The page is a stage

One title at a time deserves the whole screen — the hero you already built is sitting there showing something you didn't ask about. → B, D

The tile is the object

Don't put information beside the thing; make the thing itself open up. Most tactile, most expensive. → C

Five directions below. A is the by-the-book one and the only one that works identically on every surface. B is the cinematic one. C is the show-off. D is atmosphere, not information, and composes with any of the others. E isn't a look at all — it's the timing rule that decides whether B and C feel alive or exhausting. F is the separate, easy win on the detail page itself.

The constraint that shapes all of it: Invariant 4 — the TV renders server-composed state and holds no derived catalog state. So none of this is a client feature. Every word in every plate has to arrive from /api/tv/**, which means the real decision isn't visual, it's where the extra bytes come from (see the costs panel, far right). That is also exactly why your instinct about a jellystructure switch is right: the flag doesn't just hide a panel, it stops the backend composing and shipping the data at all.

Recommendation: A + E as one phase (plate, dwell-gated, off by default), F as a separate small phase that needs no new plumbing beyond two fields. B as a follow-up once A's payload question is settled — it reuses A's data exactly and adds only artwork. C drawn but not recommended: it reflows a lazy row on every focus move, which is the one thing invariant 11 tells us to measure before promising.

Direction A · recommended

The focus plate

A plate opens beneath the focused tile after a short dwell: title at reading size, the full meta line, every genre, three lines of synopsis, the audio/subtitle flag strip you already designed for the detail hero, and — if you've started it — where you left off. It is an overlay. Nothing reflows, no row moves, no tile resizes, and it adds zero focus stops — Down from the tile still goes to the next row, Select still opens the detail page. Placement is a rule, not a position: below the tile when there's room, beside it when there isn't. Drawing it at true scale is what surfaced this — the plate is ~380px tall, so on the 6-up browse grid (450px posters) nothing fits above or below and it has to take the nearest free side, flipping to the left edge for the last two columns. In rows, the focused row simply has to sit high enough, which it already does once you've navigated into it. Third frame — the state that matters most: a title the scanner found nothing for. The plate shrinks to what exists — no synopsis line, no flag strip, no resume row, no reserved gap — which is the same discipline R222 fixed in place for the slow-to-start note. Because it's anchored to a tile and not to a page, it behaves identically on Home, a channel page, browse, search, My List, More like this — which is the whole reason to prefer it. The second shot is the browse grid, unchanged apart from the plate. Cost: one new component, no layout risk, no new focus model. Needs: synopsis + genres + runtime + rating + resume for every item in every row.

Direction B

The hero mirrors what you're on

Stop treating the hero as an advert. When focus enters a row, the hero cross-fades — after a longer dwell than A's — into the title you are actually looking at: its backdrop, its title logo, its full synopsis. The carousel parks itself and resumes only when focus returns to the top. The pleasure of this one is that it's nearly free in UI terms: .hero-slide already renders exactly these fields. It's a data swap and a 500 ms fade, not a new component. The screen suddenly feels like it's paying attention to you. But it only exists where a hero exists — Home and channels with a configured hero. Browse, search and My List get nothing, so B is not a replacement for A, it's a layer on top of it. And a big backdrop per focus move is real image traffic, pulled from Jellyfin on the data plane. Open: does Select on a mirrored hero play that title? (It must — otherwise the hero lies.) Does the carousel ever resume while you sit in a row?

Direction C · drawn, not recommended

The tile unfolds

The focused poster becomes a landscape card in place: backdrop on top, facts beneath, a play affordance in the corner. Its neighbours slide right to make room. This is the most physical, most "designed" option and the one people notice. It is also the one that reflows a lazy row on every single focus move. Constitution invariant 11 exists because D-pad traversal on this hardware already had to be rescued once with full AOT compilation — 7–9 missed frame deadlines per pass before, 0–1 after. A width animation on the focused child, with siblings translating, is precisely the workload that regressed. It can't be specced on a drawing; it needs a measurement on the BRAVIA first. Second problem, quieter: the poster you were looking at disappears when it becomes a backdrop, so the row's shape changes under your thumb and left/right stops feeling like a shelf. Third, visible in the drawing: the card is taller than the row — 541px against the poster's 396 — so it overruns the next row's heading. Either that stays (it reads as a bug) or the page reflows vertically as well as horizontally, which doubles the cost of the thing we already can't promise. If we ever build it: only in fixed-width rows (never the 6-up grid), and the card must occupy the poster's slot exactly so nothing after it moves.

Direction D · composes with A or B

Ambience — the page takes on the title's colour

Not information: mood. The focused title's backdrop bleeds into the page background at ~16% behind a heavy blur, with a soft accent wash following it. Nothing to read, nothing to focus, no layout at all — the room just changes temperature as you move. Drawn here without a plate so you can judge it on its own. In practice it would sit under A. It's the cheapest "feels expensive" move available, and it reuses the backdrop B already needs. Skin caution: Noir is near-black with gold accents; a colour wash there reads as a stain rather than atmosphere, and R221 already found amber tints reading as warnings. Noir would take the art bleed at half strength and drop the accent wash entirely. Watch: contrast. Every tile label and row heading must still clear 4.5:1 over the brightest part of the wash — cap opacity, don't tune it per title.

Direction E · the rule, not a look

Effort earns detail: the dwell ladder

A viewer holding Right for two seconds crosses fourteen tiles. If each one fires a plate, a backdrop swap and a colour change, the screen is a strobe and the network is on fire. Every direction above lives or dies on one number, so make it three numbers instead.

0 msTile scales and rings, exactly as today. Fast traversal shows nothing else — no request, no fade.
~260 msYou stopped. The plate fades up (A). Cheap: text already in the feed, no image fetch.
~900 msYou're considering it. Backdrop arrives — hero mirror (B) and/or the ambience wash (D).
LeavingPlate dismisses in 120 ms; the hero holds the last title rather than snapping back mid-glance.

Two rules make it robust: the 900 ms tier never blocks on an image (if the backdrop isn't in cache it simply arrives late or not at all), and the neighbouring two tiles' art is prefetched at small size so the common case — one step left or right — is instant.

Rejected outright: trailer autoplay on focus. It's the obvious next step and it's wrong here. Every focus-triggered preview is a Jellyfin playback session and, at this house's bitrates, very likely a transcode — the exact outbound behaviour 183 spent a phase pacing and R231 spent a phase repairing. A viewer sweeping a row would open a dozen encode sessions nobody watches. If motion is ever wanted, it should be a pre-rendered short loop the backend produces once, not a live stream.
Also rejected: putting R222's "slow to start on this TV" note in the plate. R222 deliberately scopes that sentence to the detail page and to episode rows; at row level it would appear and vanish as you sweep, which is exactly the flicker that phase's split-responsibility design was built to avoid.
Direction F · recommended, independent

“About this title” — the detail page finally holds everything

Separate from the focus question, and cheap. The detail page today clamps the synopsis behind ▾ more and shows no runtime, no director, no studio, no first-air date, nothing about when it entered your library. All of it is already scanned and sitting in jellystructure. A quiet About section under Cast & Crew: the synopsis in full at 22px, and a two-column facts grid beside it. No focus stops — it's a reading surface, and Down still walks to More like this. Under it, the additional artwork you asked about — the backdrops and episode stills the library already holds. This is the weakest idea in the document and I'd like to argue it down: on a TV a focusable image that does nothing on Select is a dead end. It earns its place only if Select opens a full-screen viewer, which is a real feature, not a strip. Recommend shipping About now and holding the strip. Needs: runtime, director, studio/network, premiere date, date-added on the detail payload. All present in the scanner today.

What has to be true before any of this is a spec

The visual choice is the easy half. These are the answers that decide whether this is one phase or three — and two of them can only be settled by the dev side.

  1. Where do the extra bytes come from? The fork in the road. Either /api/tv/home fattens — synopsis, genres, runtime, rating, backdrop URL for every item in every row, call it ~1 KB × 40+ titles — or a per-item GET /api/tv/item/{id}/peek is fetched on dwell and cached. The first slows the cold start that R210–R213 spent four phases fixing; the second puts a request on a D-pad move. My instinct: fatten the feed but only when the flag is on, and ship the plate's text with the row it belongs to.
  2. Backdrop traffic. B and D need artwork per focused title, direct from Jellyfin. Full-size backdrops are out of the question at browse speed — this wants a small fixed width (~640) through the existing artwork proxy, a two-tile prefetch window, and no request at all below the 900 ms tier.
  3. Does C survive the hardware? Unanswerable from a drawing. Needs a real focus-sweep measurement on the stue BRAVIA before it's promised to anyone (invariant 11).
  4. Items with nothing to say. Plenty of library titles have no synopsis and no backdrop. The plate must shrink to what exists and never reserve empty space — R222's rule, and the reason the drawn plate has an "no description yet" state rather than a gap.
  5. Which surfaces are in scope? Discover/Seerr rows are TMDB-shaped, not library items; Live TV's "On now" row holds programmes, not titles. Both would need their own field mapping — I'd exclude both in phase one and say so.
  6. Where the flag lives. Per Jellyfin user in the Ravilo config (config is server-owned and synced, so it follows the viewer to every TV) — and no setting on the TV itself, per R216's standing rule. The admin card at left is drawn to that shape.

What I'd write into the spec as invariants

  • The plate renders, never computes — no client-side derivation of runtime, "18 min left", or genre order.
  • The plate adds no focus stops and cannot move Play. Down from a tile goes where it goes today.
  • Flag off ⇒ the backend composes none of it and the payload is byte-identical to today's.
  • No focus-triggered playback of any kind, ever.
